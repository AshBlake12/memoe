\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Mixture-of-Experts checkpoints are 93--98\% expert weights, and any one token
touches a small fraction of them. That makes expert weights the obvious
candidate for demotion to CXL-attached memory. We build an analytical footprint
model, a routing-trace generator, and a trace-driven simulator of a two-tier
HBM+CXL expert store, and we use them to ask when the demotion actually pays.
The answer is narrower than the premise suggests. Batching amortises the fetch
across tokens, but past a few hundred tokens a batch touches \emph{every}
expert at \emph{every} layer, at which point the HBM tier is no longer a cache
but a fixed partition whose cold half is refetched on every batch. We derive a
closed form for the batch size $B^\ast$ above which the fetch is hidden behind
compute, show that it is independent of both expert size and GPU count, and
show that it is governed almost entirely by the hit rate of the hot tier. We
then show that static popularity placement matches or beats LRU and LFU in this
regime, and that a depth-8 speculative prefetch of the head of the cold tail
removes the remaining stall entirely at batch 2048. All CXL figures use
published measured sustained bandwidth; pooled-CXL results are labelled as
modelled throughout.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}

A Mixture-of-Experts layer replaces one feed-forward network with $E$ of them
and routes each token to $k \ll E$. Capacity grows with $E$; compute grows with
$k$. DeepSeek-V3 has 671\,B parameters and activates 37\,B per token. The
consequence for memory is stark: Table~\ref{tab:footprint} shows that routed
experts are 93--98\% of the weights across four checkpoints spanning two orders
of magnitude in size, and that our footprint model reproduces the published
total and active parameter counts, which is the check that licenses everything
downstream.

Those weights sit idle. At any instant a token is using $k/E$ of them, which is
$3\%$ for DeepSeek-V3. Yet they occupy HBM, the scarcest and most expensive
memory in the system, and they set the GPU count purely by capacity rather than
by compute (Table~\ref{tab:capacity}, Fig.~\ref{fig:gpu}). CXL Type-3 expansion
offers 512\,GB or more per node at roughly a fiftieth of HBM bandwidth. The
question this paper answers is whether that trade is ever worth making.

\section{Method}

\paragraph{Footprint model.} Each routed expert is a gated FFN,
$3 d_{\text{model}} d_{\text{ff}}$ parameters. We derive the expert pool, the
pinned non-expert remainder, and the per-token active set from YAML
descriptions of each checkpoint, and validate against published counts.

\paragraph{Routing traces.} A trace records the top-$k$ expert indices per
token per layer. We synthesise traces with a controllable Zipf exponent $s$
using the Gumbel-top-$k$ trick, which draws exactly $k$ distinct experts per
token from a categorical distribution, vectorised over the batch. We also ship
architecture-agnostic forward hooks that capture the same structure from a real
checkpoint by matching router module names and verifying output width against
the expert count.

\paragraph{Simulator.} Expert instances are addressed as (layer, expert). A
placement policy decides which live in HBM; the rest live in CXL. For each
layer of each batch we compute the distinct experts required, split them into
hits and misses, cover what a speculative prefetch could have fetched within
its window, and charge the remainder against the compute of the tokens that
did hit:
\[
\text{stall} = \max\!\left(0,\; \frac{n_{\text{demand}} S}{BW} + n_{\text{demand}}\ell
- t_{\text{layer}}\, f_{\text{hit}}\right)
\]
with $t_{\text{layer}} = 6 d_{\text{model}} d_{\text{ff}} (k + k_{\text{shared}})
B / F$. CXL bandwidth and capacity scale with the GPU count, since each node
carries its own attachment.

\paragraph{Honesty constraints.} CXL bandwidth is always drawn from the
published measured sustained range of 18--52\,GB/s, never the theoretical link
rate; a unit test enforces this. CXL 3.0 pooling is pre-production, so any
configuration using it is flagged and every result derived from it carries a
modelled annotation. Bandwidth points above 52\,GB/s in
Table~\ref{tab:bandwidth} are marked extrapolated. Our compute model counts
routed-expert GEMMs only, so attention and dense layers would hide more
transfer than we credit and the reported overheads are conservative.

\section{Batching amortises, then stops}

Per-token CXL traffic falls as roughly $1/B$, because an expert is fetched once
per batch and used by many tokens (Fig.~\ref{fig:batch}, left). This is the
observation the project began from, and it is correct.

The reason it stops falling is more consequential. Table~\ref{tab:coverage}
gives the fraction of experts touched per layer per batch. At $B=256$ it is
$1.0$ for OLMoE and Mixtral and $0.99$ for Qwen3; by $B=1024$ every model
touches everything. Once coverage saturates, the resident set is not a cache in
any useful sense. There is no recency to exploit, no reuse distance to shorten.
Every non-resident expert is fetched on every batch, forever. The only thing
that matters is which experts are on which side of the partition, which is why
Section~\ref{sec:policy} finds that a placement decided once at load time is
not merely adequate but optimal.

\section{The critical batch size}

Setting the per-layer stall below $\varepsilon$ times the per-layer compute and
solving for $B$ gives
\begin{equation}
B^\ast = \frac{(1-h)\, E\, F}{BW \cdot k \cdot \varepsilon}
\label{eq:bstar}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the fraction of expert \emph{fetches} avoided by the hot tier.
Two properties of Eq.~\ref{eq:bstar} are worth stating plainly.

First, $B^\ast$ is independent of expert size $S$. A larger expert costs
proportionally more to fetch and does proportionally more work once fetched;
the two cancel exactly. Mixtral's 336\,MB experts are not the problem people
assume they are.

Second, $B^\ast$ is independent of GPU count, because compute and per-node CXL
bandwidth both scale with the deployment. Scaling out does not buy a way past
the constraint.

What remains is the hit rate, and it enters linearly.
Table~\ref{tab:critical} makes the design requirement concrete: at $h=0.9$,
even OLMoE needs a batch of 61\,k tokens, which is not a serving regime. At
$h=0.99$ it needs 6\,k, which is reachable in a large prefill. DeepSeek-V3
needs $h=0.999$ before it enters the same range. Decode-sized batches never
qualify, for any model here. Table~\ref{tab:crossover} confirms this
empirically against the simulator, and Fig.~\ref{fig:crossover} shows the
crossing.

The practical reading is that expert offload over CXL is a prefill technique,
not a decode technique, and that the entire engineering problem reduces to
building a hot tier with a very high fetch hit rate.

\section{What sets the hit rate}
\label{sec:policy}

The ceiling on hit rate at a given capacity is the popularity coverage curve
(Fig.~\ref{fig:coverage}): the fraction of routings served by keeping the
hottest $x\%$ of experts. Its shape is set by router skew, and
Table~\ref{tab:skew} shows the whole approach standing or falling on that
skew. At $s=0$, uniform routing, a 50\% resident tier serves 50\% of routings
and tiering buys exactly its capacity fraction. Every positive result in this
paper is contingent on real routers being skewed, and load-balancing losses
used during MoE training push directly against skew. Measuring this on a real
checkpoint is the most important experiment we have not yet run, and the hooks
exist for it.

Given a coverage curve, the policy question is what to keep. We compare static
popularity placement, a per-layer-quota variant, LRU, and sampled LFU. At
serving batch sizes the static policies match or beat the dynamic ones, for the
reason given above: with full coverage there is no recency signal left. This is
a fortunate result for CXL specifically, because a static placement is decided
once at load time and costs nothing on the critical path, whereas an LRU over
tens of thousands of expert slots would not be implementable there anyway.

\section{Prefetching the cold tail}

The residual stall comes from cold experts that are needed anyway. A
prefetcher can hide it if it names them early enough. Prefetching the
\emph{hottest} experts is useless, since those are exactly what the hot tier
already holds; the prefetcher must target the head of the cold tail.
Table~\ref{tab:prefetch} and Fig.~\ref{fig:prefetch} show the effect at 90\%
residency and batch 2048: OLMoE falls from 2.03 to zero overhead at depth 4
with a 10\% width, and Qwen3-235B from 4.98 to zero at depth 8. Depth widens
the transfer window linearly, and beyond the point where the window covers the
predicted set, further depth is free but useless.

This prefetcher uses only a static popularity prior. A predictor conditioned on
layer $L-1$ routing should do better, and we have not built one.

\section{Threats to validity}

Traces are synthetic, Zipf $s=1.0$ with mild drift; this is the weakest
assumption in the work and Section~\ref{sec:policy} quantifies how much rests
on it. Pooled CXL 3.0 numbers are modelled, as no shipping switch silicon
exists to measure. We model bandwidth and capacity but not contention, so
concurrent KV-cache traffic over the same link is unaccounted for. The compute
model omits attention and dense layers, which makes our overheads conservative.
Finally, we simulate a placement and transfer model, not a kernel: a real
implementation must also solve overlap scheduling and allocator fragmentation,
neither of which we address.

\section{Conclusion}

CXL memory expansion for MoE inference is not the general capacity win the
framing invites. It is a prefill-regime technique whose viability is set by a
single closed-form condition, Eq.~\ref{eq:bstar}, in which the hot-tier hit
rate is the only lever with orders of magnitude in it. Within that regime the
right design is unglamorous and cheap: static popularity placement decided at
load time, plus a deep speculative prefetch aimed at the head of the cold tail.
Whether the regime is reachable at all depends on how skewed real routers are,
which is a measurement, not an argument.
