\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Mixture-of-Experts models activate a small slice of their parameters per token,
yet every expert has to sit in GPU memory. DeepSeek-V3 activates about 37 billion
of its 671 billion parameters and still needs sixteen 80~GB GPUs just to hold its
weights. We asked whether a larger, slower tier such as CXL-attached DRAM can hold
the idle experts, and what that costs. To answer it we characterised the tier in
DRAMSim3 and gem5, captured 258{,}629 tokens of real routing from OLMoE-1B-7B,
built a trace-driven simulator, an event-driven SystemC model and a closed form
for the critical batch size, and built MEMoE-RT, a runtime that runs real
checkpoints with their experts outside GPU memory.

The measurements pushed us away from the usual popularity-based tiering design.
At serving batch sizes a batch touches nearly every expert, fetch hit rate equals
the resident fraction regardless of routing skew, and a placement profiled on one
workload can serve another worse than random placement. What is left is a
capacity decision and a transfer-scheduling problem. MEMoE-RT, built on that
design, runs OLMoE-1B-7B with every expert off-GPU at a prefill batch of
16{,}384 tokens, keeping 91.2\% of resident throughput in 7.27~GB instead of
17.73~GB. It also runs DeepSeek-V2-Lite (31.4~GB) on a 23~GB GPU at 7{,}241
tokens per second. In a serving loop the same offload adds 7\% to prefill time
and 525\% to decode time, so our main recommendation is to use expert offload on
prefill workers and keep decode resident. A planning tool carries the method to
other checkpoints and GPUs: from a model config and two measurements of the card,
it predicts what fits and where the crossover sits, and it places DeepSeek-V2-Lite's
crossover on the A10 inside the interval we measured.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem}
% =====================================================================

A Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) layer replaces one feed-forward network with many
experts, and a router picks a few of them per token. Compute per token stays
roughly flat while parameter count grows. That works well on paper. In deployment,
every expert still has to be addressable by the GPU, even though almost none of
them are in use at any moment.

Table~\ref{tab:footprint} shows how lopsided this is. In the four checkpoints we
modelled, routed experts are 93.1\% to 97.5\% of all parameters. DeepSeek-V3 needs
sixteen 80~GB devices to hold 1{,}249~GiB of weights, and 1{,}218~GiB of that is
experts. Most of those GPUs are there for memory, not for compute.

device, plus 512~GB of CXL memory per device in the last column.}
\label{tab:footprint}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}

Model & Weights & Expert pool & Experts & GPUs, HBM only & GPUs, HBM+CXL \\
\midrule

Mixtral-8x7B~\cite{mixtral}    & 87.0   & 84.0   & 96.6\% & 2  & 1 \\

Compute Express Link (CXL)~\cite{cxl} is one way out. A CXL Type-3 expander
attaches ordinary DRAM over a PCIe-class link and can add hundreds of gigabytes
per node for much less than HBM costs. It is also far slower. The obvious design
is to keep popular experts in HBM and fetch the rest on demand. We set out to
build that, and our own measurements talked us out of it.

\subsection{Architecture model}

Everything in this report rests on a model of two memory tiers attached to one
compute device (Table~\ref{tab:archmodel}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{The two tiers as modelled. HBM figures are HBM3e specifications at the
effective rate we use throughout. CXL figures are our DRAMSim3 measurements at
DDR4-3200 plus a controller and link allowance.}
\label{tab:archmodel}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
 & HBM3e (fast tier) & CXL Type-3 DDR4 (capacity tier) \\
\midrule
Attach            & on-package, on the GPU  & PCIe/CXL link, off-package \\
Capacity/device   & 80 GB modelled          & 512 GB per card, 2 TB pooled \\
Rated bandwidth   & 3{,}350 GB/s            & 25.6 GB/s per channel \\
Effective bandwidth & 3{,}350 GB/s          & 16.88 GB/s measured, 67.5 GB/s for 4 channels \\
Loaded latency    & $\sim$350 ns            & 179.4 ns device, $\sim$300 ns total \\

Expandable, poolable & no                   & yes (pooling needs a CXL 3.0 switch) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The bandwidth row is the design problem. A tier 50 to 198 times slower than HBM
is hopeless for anything read on every decode step and fine for anything read
once per batch. Section~\ref{sec:kv} turns that into a rule based on arithmetic
intensity: expert weights can move, active KV cache cannot.

Latency matters less than it looks. The $\sim$300~ns CXL figure is 179.4~ns of
measured device latency plus about 120~ns for the controller and link hop, inside
the 200--400~ns range reported for early CXL devices. When a whole expert moves
in one transfer, latency is 0.16\% of transfer time and the tier behaves as a
pure bandwidth device. Section~\ref{sec:granularity} shows where that stops being
true.

\subsection{What a CXL-class DRAM back end sustains}

We didn't want to quote CXL bandwidth from a datasheet. A Type-3 expander is DRAM
behind a controller, so we simulated the DRAM in DRAMSim3~\cite{dramsim3} and
measured the rate it sustained while busy. Each trace was a 12~MB sequential read,
the size of one OLMoE expert, issued as 196{,}608 accesses of 64 bytes.
DRAMSim3's headline bandwidth divides by the whole cycle budget including idle
cycles, so we compute sustained bandwidth from the average interarrival time
instead.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{DRAMSim3, DDR4 x8, 12~MB sequential expert read (four of the six grades
swept).}
\label{tab:dram}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Grade & Rated & Sustained & Share of rating & Loaded latency \\
\midrule
DDR4-1866 & 14.9 GB/s & 12.76 GB/s & 85.5\% & 237.9 ns \\
DDR4-2400 & 19.2 GB/s & 14.96 GB/s & 77.9\% & 202.9 ns \\
DDR4-2933 & 23.5 GB/s & 15.65 GB/s & 66.7\% & 192.9 ns \\
DDR4-3200 & 25.6 GB/s & \textbf{16.88 GB/s} & 65.9\% & \textbf{179.4 ns} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

We use 16.88~GB/s everywhere instead of the rated figure. The trend matters as
well: the share of the rating actually reached drops from 85.5\% to 65.9\% as the
grade gets faster, because timing constraints such as refresh don't scale with the
data rate. An expander should be sized by sustained bandwidth, and the gap to the
rating widens with faster parts.

\subsection{Channel scaling in gem5}
\label{sec:channels}

The four-channel figure of 67.5~GB/s is the measured single channel times four.
DRAMSim3 couldn't measure it directly, because its trace driver issues at most one
request per cycle and starves the extra channels. gem5~\cite{gem5} has no such
limit, so we ran a linear traffic generator through a CommMonitor into $N$
interleaved DDR4-2400 \texttt{MemCtrl} channels (gem5 v22.1, 64-byte reads,
10~ms simulated, 128-byte bus).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{Sustained read bandwidth in gem5 against channel count.}
\label{tab:channels}
\begin{tabular}{rrr}
\toprule
Channels & Sustained & Ratio to one channel \\
\midrule
1 & 13.86 GB/s & 1.000 \\

8\% of DRAMSim3's 14.96~GB/s for the same DDR4-2400 grade. Two simulators with
different DRAM models land in the same place, well below the 19.2~GB/s rating.

\section{How real routing behaves}
\label{sec:routing}

Every tiering argument depends on how skewed routing is, so we measured it. We
hooked the router of OLMoE-1B-7B-0924-Instruct and recorded the selected experts
for every token across four workloads (prose, maths, code and chat), 258{,}629
tokens in total.

\subsection{Coverage saturates at small batches}
\label{sec:coverage}

Tiering only pays if a batch leaves some experts untouched. In the captured
traces, a batch of 64 tokens already touches 76\% to 90\% of experts per layer,
and by 1{,}024 tokens it touches 97.3\% for code and 99.4\% to 100\% for the
others. Past that point every non-resident expert is fetched on every batch, so
the fast tier is no longer a cache. It is a fixed partition. Real serving sits in
that zone: prefill processes thousands of tokens at once, and continuous batching
typically runs 64 to 256 sequences in decode.

\subsection{Popularity buys nothing}
\label{sec:policy}

If every expert gets touched, ranking experts by popularity shouldn't help. We
checked with the simulator, sweeping synthetic routing from uniform to more skewed
than anything we measured, at 90\% residency.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{Simulated fetch hit rate at 90\% residency against routing skew.}
\label{tab:skewsweep}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Batch & $s=0.0$ & $s=0.6$ & $s=1.0$ & $s=1.5$ \\
\midrule
64    & 0.899 & 0.900 & 0.907 & 0.927 \\

From batch 512 up, the fetch hit rate equals the resident fraction at every skew,
and static placement, LRU and sampled LFU give the same number. That is good news
for implementation: static placement is decided once at load time and costs
nothing per token, so the cheapest policy is as good as any.

\subsection{The wrong profile is worse than none}
\label{sec:profile}

Placement can't help at scale, but it can hurt at smaller batches, where the
routing hit rate (the share of token routings served from HBM) still depends on
which experts are resident. We took the top quarter of experts for each workload
and measured overlap between workloads, with a split-half control inside each
workload.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{subfigure}{0.48\linewidth}
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{r2_overlap.png}
  \caption{Hot-set overlap. Chance is 25\%.}
\end{subfigure}\hfill
\begin{subfigure}{0.48\linewidth}
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{r3_penalty.png}
  \caption{Routing hit rate at 50\% residency. Random is 0.500.}
\end{subfigure}
\caption{Hot expert sets form two groups: prose with chat, and maths with code.

\label{fig:overlap}

Split-half overlap averages 84.9\% (75.0\% to 94.9\%), so hot sets are stable
within a workload. Across workloads, maths and code overlap 50.0\%, but prose and
code overlap only 15.2\%, below the 25\% you'd expect by chance. At 50\%
residency, a placement profiled on the workload it serves reaches a routing hit
rate of 0.756 on average. A mismatched one averages 0.519, barely above random,
and the worst pair (profiled on prose, serving code) reaches 0.383, which is
worse than random. For mixed traffic there is no good static placement, so the
resident set should be chosen by capacity alone.

\section{The capacity model}
\label{sec:capacity}
% =====================================================================

With skew and policy out of the picture, two decisions remain: how much fast
memory to keep, and how early to start each transfer. The first has a closed form.
Let $E$ be experts per layer, $k$ experts activated per token, $h$ the share of
fetches the resident tier avoids, $F$ per-GPU compute in FLOP/s, $BW$ tier
bandwidth, and $\varepsilon$ the tolerated stall as a fraction of compute. Fetch
traffic per layer scales with $(1-h)E$ times expert size, and compute per layer
scales with $Bk$ times expert size. Expert size cancels, and transfer fits inside
the stall budget once $B > B^{*}$:

\begin{equation}
B^{*} = \frac{(1-h)\,E\,F}{BW \cdot k \cdot \varepsilon}
\label{eq:bstar}
\end{equation}

$B^{*}$ doesn't depend on expert size, since a bigger expert costs more to move
and does proportionally more work once moved. It doesn't depend on GPU count
either, because compute and per-node bandwidth scale together under tensor
parallelism. Adding GPUs doesn't move the boundary.

\keyresult{\textbf{Checking the closed form.} Calibrating $F$ on the fully
resident OLMoE baseline gives 33.7~TFLOPS. With $h=0.3$, $E=64$, $k=8$,
$BW=40.8$~GB/s and $\varepsilon=1$, Equation~\ref{eq:bstar} gives
$B^{*}=4{,}626$ tokens. From the runtime's own measurements, the time to
stream the bytes it actually moved at 70\% offload equals resident compute time at
4{,}645 tokens, a 0.5\% difference. The SystemC model, which shares no code with either, finds
per-layer transfer and compute time equal at 4{,}626 (\S\ref{sec:systemc}).}

% =====================================================================
\section{Experts, not the KV cache}
\label{sec:kv}
% =====================================================================

The KV cache is the other thing that eats HBM, so we checked whether it belongs
on the slow tier too. It doesn't. A tier of bandwidth $BW$ feeding a device at
$F$ FLOP/s is balanced at $F/BW$ FLOPs per byte, and anything below that ratio is
bandwidth-bound. Each fetched expert serves $Bk/E$ tokens, so expert intensity
\emph{grows with the batch}. Each KV element is read once per decode step by
$n_\text{heads}/n_\text{kv heads}$ query heads, so KV intensity is \emph{fixed},
and batching can't help because every sequence has its own cache.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{Arithmetic intensity in FLOPs per byte against a four-channel CXL tier,
whose balance point is 5{,}861.}
\label{tab:kv}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
Model & KV intensity & Balance point & Batch at which experts balance \\
\midrule
OLMoE-1B-7B     & 1.0  & 5{,}861 & 46{,}886 \\
Mixtral-8x7B    & 4.0  & 5{,}861 & 23{,}443 \\
Qwen3-235B-A22B & 16.0 & 5{,}861 & 93{,}772 \\
DeepSeek-V3     & 1.0  & 5{,}861 & 166{,}706 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Qwen3 has the best KV intensity in the set thanks to 16-to-1 grouped-query
attention, and it is still 366 times below the balance point. In concrete terms,
one 32k-context Qwen3 sequence reads 5.875~GiB of KV per decode step. That takes
1.883~ms on HBM3e (531 steps per second) and 93.455~ms on a four-channel CXL tier
(11 steps per second), a 49.6-fold slowdown. Active KV has to stay in HBM.

% =====================================================================
\section{MEMoE-RT}
\label{sec:system}
% =====================================================================

Everything so far is analysis or simulation. To test it, we built a runtime that
runs the real checkpoint with its experts somewhere else.

\subsection{Design}

Each finding above removes a component. Since coverage saturates, every expert in
a layer will be needed, so there is nothing to predict and the only problem is
starting the transfer early enough. MEMoE-RT therefore moves every non-resident
expert of layer $L{+}d$ as one contiguous transfer, issued $d$ layers ahead, on a
dedicated CUDA copy stream that overlaps attention compute on the main stream.
Since popularity doesn't matter, the resident set is just the lowest expert
indices, with no ranking. And since latency only matters for small transfers
(\S\ref{sec:granularity}), we move whole layers. We checked the link before
building: 4~MB transfers reach 33.05~GB/s, 12~MB reach 36.67~GB/s, and larger ones
plateau at 40.81~GB/s. A layer's offloaded experts are hundreds of megabytes, well
onto the plateau.

Offloaded experts live in pinned host memory, contiguous per layer, and a ring of
$d{+}1$ GPU staging buffers receives them (Figures~\ref{fig:arch}
and~\ref{fig:timeline}). Layer $L$ uses slot $L \bmod (d{+}1)$, so the prefetch
issued at layer $L$ lands in the slot layer $L{-}1$ has just finished with. All
waiting happens GPU-side through stream events, never host synchronisation, and
stall time comes from CUDA events recorded around each wait. A second code path
handles checkpoints that keep experts as a \texttt{ModuleList}, such as
DeepSeek-V2-Lite: it leaves the model's forward pass alone, points each offloaded
expert's weights at a fixed view into the staging ring, and drives transfers from
hooks on each MoE block.

\subsection{PCIe as a stand-in for CXL}

Host DRAM over PCIe is the standard stand-in for a CXL tier: a large, slow,
byte-addressable tier behind a link. At PCIe 5.0 x16 its measured plateau
of 40.8~GB/s sits between our single-channel CXL figure (16.88~GB/s) and the
four-channel one (67.5~GB/s). MEMoE-RT moves whole layers in bulk, which depends
on bandwidth rather than per-access latency or cache coherence, so PCIe reproduces
the structure of the problem.

\subsection{Correctness}

Timing numbers mean nothing if offload changes the output, so every runtime result
passes a check first. Running with 25\% and 100\% of experts resident on
identical input gives a maximum absolute logit difference of exactly zero, so the
transfer path changes nothing. Against the unmodified Hugging Face model, argmax
agreement on random tokens is 92.8\% on the Blackwell GPU, with 99.4\% of tiered
top-1 tokens inside the reference top-5 on the A10. The gap is bfloat16 rounding
in our re-implemented MoE forward pass (CUDA \texttt{index\_add\_} accumulates in
nondeterministic order). It is identical at every residency level, and half of
random-token positions have a top-1/top-2 logit gap under 0.5.

\section{Simulation framework: HBM-only against HBM+CXL}

Every quantitative result compares two configurations of the same model on the
same hardware. \textbf{HBM-only} has every weight, including the full expert pool,
in GPU memory. \textbf{HBM+CXL} keeps only the non-expert weights and a resident
fraction of experts in HBM, and streams the rest one layer ahead of use. Four
implementations evaluate that comparison, and none of them share code
(Table~\ref{tab:framework}). DRAMSim3 and gem5 supply the tier constants to all
four. The interactive dashboard (\texttt{results/dashboard.html}, a single
self-contained file) shows every result in this report and lets a reader enter
their own model and hardware into Equation~\ref{eq:bstar}.

\section{What the runtime measures}

\subsection{Lookahead depth, and a correction}
\label{sec:depth}

Our simulator had recommended prefetching 4 to 8 layers ahead. The hardware

rather than our original recommendation.

\subsection{How much transfer hides under compute}
\label{sec:overlap}

The closed form assumes transfers hide completely under compute. Fitting measured
wall time across the residency sweep to $t = t_\text{compute} + \kappa \cdot
t_\text{transfer}$ gives $\kappa = 0.40 \pm 0.07$ (Figure~\ref{fig:overlapfit}):
about 60\% of transfer time hides and 40\% is exposed, due to contention between
the copy engine and compute. The ideal model overstates throughput by up to 40\%
where transfer and compute are balanced. Scaling the predicted exposure by
$\kappa$ brings it in line with the hardware across the sweep.

\section{Memory expansion, pooling and transfer granularity}
\label{sec:pooling}
% =====================================================================

\textbf{Expansion} is the case measured throughout this report: one expander per
node holding that node's expert pool turns a capacity problem into a bandwidth
problem. Table~\ref{tab:footprint} gives the effect at scale: DeepSeek-V3 goes from
sixteen GPUs to three with 512~GB of CXL memory each. MEMoE-RT on DeepSeek-V2-Lite
is the small-scale version of the same thing, running a model that otherwise
wouldn't run on that GPU at all.

\textbf{Pooling} puts one copy of the cold experts behind a switch shared by $N$
nodes. The pool then needs $1/N$ of the capacity that dedicated expanders would,
but all $N$ nodes share one link, so per-node bandwidth falls as $1/N$. Because
$B^{*}$ is inversely proportional to bandwidth, the batch each node needs to stay
above the crossover grows as $N$ (Table~\ref{tab:pooling}).

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering\small
\caption{Pooling Qwen3-235B-A22B experts behind a 67.5~GB/s pool, $h=0.9$. Pooling
figures are modelled: CXL 3.0 switches are not yet available to measure.}
\label{tab:pooling}
\begin{tabular}{rrrr}
\toprule
Nodes & Capacity saved & Per-node bandwidth & Required batch $B^{*}$ \\
\midrule
1  &        0 GB & 67.50 GB/s &    93{,}771 \\
2  &      423 GB & 33.75 GB/s &   187{,}543 \\
4  & 1{,}269 GB & 16.88 GB/s &   375{,}087 \\
8  & 2{,}961 GB &  8.44 GB/s &   750{,}174 \\
16 & 6{,}345 GB &  4.22 GB/s & 1{,}500{,}349 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

At sixteen nodes the pool saves 6.3~TB, but each node would need a 1.5-million-token
batch, which no deployment runs. Two to four nodes save real capacity while keeping
the required batch closer to large prefill batches. Two things would change this
tradeoff. A pool whose link bandwidth grows with membership (CXL 3.0 switching with
multiple upstream ports) keeps $B^{*}$ constant, but that hardware is
not yet shipping, so our code marks every configuration that assumes it as
modelled (\texttt{measured: false}). Interleaving across several expanders also
raises per-node bandwidth, and our gem5 result supports near-linear gains up to
four channels (\S\ref{sec:channels}).

\textbf{Transfer granularity.}
\label{sec:granularity}
At 300~ns access latency and 67.5~GB/s, the share of transfer time spent on
latency rather than moving bytes is 0.16\% for a whole 12~MB expert, 3.72\% for
512~KB chunks, 23.6\% for 64~KB and 83.2\% for 4~KB. Anything that breaks
experts into pieces smaller than about 512~KB turns a bandwidth problem into a
latency problem. There is a second cost too. Saturating a tier of bandwidth $BW$
at latency $L$ with requests of size $S$ needs $BW \cdot L / S$ requests in flight,
roughly 320 for a 67.5~GB/s tier at 300~ns with 64-byte accesses. Moving a
whole layer as one DMA transfer avoids both. Our PCIe measurements show the same
shape: 33.05~GB/s at 4~MB, rising to the 40.81~GB/s plateau.

\subsection{An event-driven model in SystemC}
\label{sec:systemc}

The closed form was calibrated against the runtime's resident baseline, so those
two aren't fully independent. We therefore wrote the tiered path again as a small
SystemC TLM-2.0 model that shares no code with either. A \texttt{MemoryTier}
target returns latency plus bytes over bandwidth for each transaction. A
\texttt{CopyEngine} issues one blocking transport per layer carrying all of that
layer's non-resident experts, and blocks when the staging ring is full. A
\texttt{ComputeEngine} consumes layers in order and adds up the time it waits for

\subsection{The Linux CXL software path in QEMU}
\label{sec:qemu}

We also checked that the Linux CXL stack can manage this kind of tier, using QEMU
9.1 with an emulated Type-3 volatile memory device (2~GB) under an Ubuntu 24.04
guest. On the stock distribution kernel everything up to region commit worked:
CXL control was negotiated through ACPI \texttt{\_OSC}, the drivers bound, and the
root, port and endpoint decoders enumerated with correct sizes. Committing a region
then failed with \texttt{Failed to synchronize CPU cache state}.

\begin{sloppypar}
The cause is a safety check, not missing support. Before committing a region the
kernel must invalidate CPU caches over the range, and it gates that on
\texttt{cpu\_cache\_has\_invalidate\_memregion()}, which returns false whenever
\texttt{X86\_FEATURE\_HYPERVISOR} is set, i.e.\ inside any virtual machine. The
kernel has a documented test bypass, \texttt{CONFIG\_CXL\_REGION\_INVALIDATION\_TEST},
which distribution kernels leave off. We built Linux 6.8 with it enabled, without
root, and the region commits: \texttt{region0} is live at \texttt{0x490000000},
size 2~GB, one interleave way targeting \texttt{decoder2.0} in \texttt{ram} mode,
and the kernel logs \texttt{Bypassing cpu\_cache\_invalidate\_memregion() for
testing}.
\end{sloppypar}

This validates the software path a CXL tier depends on: discovery, decoder
programming, region creation and commit. The performance numbers in this report
come from DRAMSim3, gem5 and hardware.

\section{Related work}
% =====================================================================

Expert offloading is a crowded area. The systems split mostly by the batch size
they were designed for, which is the axis $B^{*}$ describes. \emph{Predict and
cache} systems target single-sequence decoding: Mixtral-offloading keeps an LRU
cache of experts and speculatively prefetches a layer or two ahead~\cite{mixoff},
MoE-Infinity adds request-level activation tracing~\cite{moeinf}, and ProMoE,
HOBBIT and AdapMoE add learned prediction, mixed precision and expert skipping.
Fiddler moves activations to the CPU and runs experts there, which it notes pays
off when the batch is small~\cite{fiddler}. \emph{Throughput pipelines} work at
large batches: ZeRO-Inference offloads layer weights with prefetch~\cite{dsinf},
FlexGen schedules across GPU, CPU and disk~\cite{flexgen}, and MoE-Lightning adds
a CPU-GPU-I/O pipeline with a Hierarchical Roofline Model~\cite{moelight}. llama.cpp's
\texttt{--n-cpu-moe} option pins whole expert tensors to host memory by layer,
with no ranking at all.

Our measurements explain why the first group's machinery stops paying above
$B^{*}$. Coverage saturates, so prediction becomes trivial. Hit rate equals
residency, so LRU, LFU and static placement give the same result. And Fiddler's
condition that activations are small compared with expert weights is linear in
batch size, so it flips at a batch set by the same quantities as $B^{*}$.

Several of MEMoE-RT's pieces have precedent. Layer-granular prefetch on a separate
stream is what ZeRO-Inference does, static unranked placement is what llama.cpp
does, and using an analytical model to choose a policy is the idea behind
MoE-Lightning's roofline model. What we add is the boundary itself: a closed form
for the batch size at which expert offload stops costing throughput, independent
of expert size and GPU count, that someone can evaluate for hardware they don't
own, and that agrees with our runtime and SystemC model on OLMoE and with the
measured crossover of DeepSeek-V2-Lite on the A10. We also give a
measured reason why unranked static placement is the right choice at serving
scale. Published speedups from these systems aren't directly comparable with our
retention figures, since each is measured against a different baseline.

\section{Recommendations}
\label{sec:recommendations}
% =====================================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Put expert weights on the capacity tier, keep active KV cache in HBM.}
      What decides it is arithmetic intensity, not size: expert intensity grows
      with batch size, KV intensity doesn't.
\item \textbf{Use expert offload for prefill.} At prefill batch sizes the whole
      expert pool can live off-GPU for under 10\% of throughput. At decode batch
      sizes it costs most of it. In disaggregated serving, offload on prefill
      workers and keep decode workers resident.
\item \textbf{Size HBM with Equation~\ref{eq:bstar}, on measured card figures.}
      Measure the link and calibrate compute on the target GPU, then choose the
      resident fraction so the batches you run sit above $B^{*}$
      (\texttt{scripts/plan\_offload.py} does both). Derate by
      $\kappa \approx 0.4$ for transfer that doesn't hide under compute.
\item \textbf{Don't build a popularity-based tiering engine.} At serving batch
      sizes fetch hit rate equals residency at every skew, and a placement
      profiled on the wrong workload does worse than random.
\item \textbf{Move one contiguous transfer per layer, one layer ahead.} Depth 1
      matches depth 8 on throughput with 41\% less staging memory, and chunks
      below about 512~KB become latency-bound.
\item \textbf{Specify expanders by sustained bandwidth.} Bulk reads reach only
      66\% to 86\% of the DIMM rating, and faster grades fall further short.
\item \textbf{Keep pools small until switch bandwidth scales with membership.}
      Two to four nodes per pool today.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

A slow memory tier can hold Mixture-of-Experts weights: during prefill, not during
decode, and not because of caching. Coverage saturates, so the fast tier is a
partition, and every popularity-based policy collapses to the same hit rate. What
remains is a capacity decision, which has a closed form, and a scheduling problem,
which moving whole layers one step ahead solves.

MEMoE-RT shows this on real hardware twice. On OLMoE-1B-7B, 93\% of the parameters
sit outside GPU memory at 91.2\% of resident throughput for a 2.44$\times$ memory
reduction, and the closed form, the runtime measurements and an independent SystemC
model agree on the crossover to within 0.5\%. On DeepSeek-V2-Lite, a 31.4~GB model
runs on a 23~GB GPU at 7{,}241 tokens per second, with its crossover where the
closed form puts it. Because the boundary depends on bandwidth and compute rather than expert
size or GPU count, the same analysis applies to a CXL expander once its measured
bandwidth is substituted.
